\chapter{EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS}

This chapter presents the experimental design used to evaluate AIM-RGL in simulated MI-CBT counseling dialogue. The purpose of the experiment is to examine whether reward-guided lookahead search can improve therapeutic response quality when compared with baseline response generation methods. The chapter describes the experimental conditions, including the simulated client setup, compared systems, lookahead configurations, and reward model backbones. It also explains the evaluation protocol and metrics used to assess therapeutic skill quality, MI fidelity, therapeutic alliance, and reward model performance. The chapter reports therapeutic response quality across systems and lookahead depths, together with the one-step reward model backbone evaluation.

\section{Experimental Design and Conditions}

\subsection{Experiment Overview}

This experiment is designed to evaluate AIM-RGL in simulated MI-CBT counseling dialogue. The main purpose is to examine whether reward-guided lookahead search can improve therapeutic response generation when the simulated client shows resistance, ambivalence, or changing readiness for structured intervention. In this setting, a response is not evaluated only by whether it appears appropriate in the current turn, but also by whether it is expected to support a better near-future therapeutic trajectory.

The experiment compares AIM-RGL with selected baseline therapist systems under the same simulated client scenarios. Each system receives the same client profile, cognitive conceptualization information, difficulty setting, and dialogue context. The resulting therapist-client conversations are evaluated using counseling-specific metrics, including therapeutic skill quality, MI fidelity, and therapeutic alliance. The experiment also examines how lookahead depth affects therapeutic response quality, MI-CBT technique selection and diversity, and technique selection across early, middle, and late conversation stages. Reward model backbones are evaluated separately for reward prediction.

The experiment is guided by the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does reward-guided lookahead search improve therapeutic competence, MI fidelity, and therapeutic alliance compared with baseline approaches?

    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How do different lookahead horizons (1-step, 2-step, and 3-step) affect reward-guided MI-CBT response quality?

    \item \textbf{RQ3:} How does lookahead depth affect MI-CBT technique selection and diversity in reward-guided response generation?

    \item \textbf{RQ4:} How does lookahead depth influence MI-CBT technique selection across conversation stages (Early, Middle, Late)?
\end{itemize}

The corresponding hypotheses are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{H1:} Reward-guided lookahead search will improve therapeutic competence, MI fidelity, and therapeutic alliance compared with baseline approaches.

    \item \textbf{H2:} Therapeutic response quality, measured through therapeutic competence, MI fidelity, and therapeutic alliance, will differ across one-step, two-step, and three-step lookahead horizons.

    \item \textbf{H3:} MI-CBT technique selection and diversity will vary with lookahead depth in reward-guided response generation.

    \item \textbf{H4:} The distribution of selected MI-CBT techniques across early, middle, and late conversation stages will vary with lookahead depth.
\end{itemize}

This experiment is limited to simulated therapist-client dialogue. It does not involve real patients or human clinical participants, and it does not measure real clinical treatment outcomes.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}

The experiment includes three main experimental factors: therapist system, lookahead horizon, and reward model backbone. The therapist system comparison is used to evaluate whether AIM-RGL improves therapeutic response quality compared with selected baseline systems. The lookahead horizon comparison is used to examine whether considering more future client reactions improves response selection. The reward model backbone comparison is used to evaluate reward prediction performance.

The experiment compares Standard, CAMEL, LLaMA Base, AIM-RGL SFT, and AIM-RGL SFT+GRPO variants trained using D1, D2, and D3 reward models. The Standard baseline uses GPT-4o-mini with a fixed therapist prompt, provided in Appendix~\ref{app:standard-prompt}. CAMEL is included as a baseline counseling agent for comparison \cite{lee2024cactus}. AIM-RGL is the proposed framework, which uses specialist therapist agents, client simulation, reward-guided lookahead search, reward model training, and policy optimization.

LLaMA Base provides the policy baseline before task-specific fine-tuning. AIM-RGL SFT denotes the supervised fine-tuned policy, and AIM-RGL SFT+GRPO denotes subsequent reward-guided policy optimization. The D1, D2, and D3 variants use reward models trained with the corresponding lookahead datasets. LLaMA Base, AIM-RGL SFT, and all GRPO variants share the technique-selection and JSON response prompt provided in Appendix~\ref{app:policy-prompt}.

For AIM-RGL, the lookahead horizon is varied using one-step, two-step, and three-step lookahead settings. These are not treated as separate system names, but as different configurations of the same reward-guided lookahead mechanism. In one-step lookahead, each candidate therapist response is followed by one simulated client response before scoring. In two-step lookahead, the system expands the trajectory further to estimate a longer short-term therapeutic effect. In three-step lookahead, the system examines whether deeper trajectory evaluation provides additional improvement in response quality.

\subsection{Dataset and Client Simulation Setup}

The experiment uses simulated client scenarios based on the Patient-\(\Psi\)-CM dataset \cite{wang2024patientpsi}. The dataset contains CBT-based cognitive models and patient cases. Each case includes cognitive conceptualization information such as the client’s situation, automatic thoughts, emotions, behaviors, coping strategies, relevant history, intermediate beliefs, and core beliefs. These elements are used to ground the simulated client’s responses and maintain consistency across multi-turn dialogue.

The client simulator generates first-person client responses based on the current conversation history, the client’s cognitive conceptualization profile, and the client’s current stage of change. The stage of change is represented using three states: precontemplation, contemplation, and preparation. Precontemplation represents a resistant client who may minimize the problem, reject feedback, or show little interest in change. Contemplation represents an ambivalent client who recognizes both reasons for and against change. Preparation represents a client who is more open to structured reflection, planning, or CBT-style intervention.

Client difficulty is represented using easy, normal, and hard settings. Difficulty controls how frequently the client's openness level and stage of change are re-evaluated during the dialogue. Easy clients are expected to become more open more quickly, normal clients change more gradually, and hard clients remain resistant for longer. The openness judge periodically re-evaluates the simulated client's openness level and stage of change based on the recent conversation history. For easy clients, openness is re-evaluated every two turns. For normal clients, it is re-evaluated every four turns. For hard clients, it is re-evaluated every six turns. This interval-based design allows the simulation to represent different levels of resistance persistence and readiness development.

GPT-4o-mini is used for candidate therapist response generation and client simulation during data construction. In AIM-RGL, the therapist-side generation module includes specialist agents for open-ended questioning, reflection, affirmation, summarization, and CBT intervention. These agents generate candidate responses before the reward-guided lookahead search stage. The reward model is trained to predict scalar therapeutic reward scores for candidate responses or trajectories. GPT-4o is used as a reward judge and evaluator for therapeutic quality dimensions, including therapeutic alliance, MI fidelity, and CBT competence. The compared systems use the same client scenario and simulation setup.

\subsection{Reward Model and Policy Training Configuration}

This subsection describes the training configurations for reward modeling, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and GRPO policy optimization. The reward model is trained before policy optimization because it provides the scalar therapeutic reward signal used to score candidate therapist responses during reward-guided lookahead search and GRPO. The methodological details are described in Chapter 3; this subsection specifies the datasets, data balancing, model backbone, and training settings used in the experiments.

\subsubsection{Reward Model Training}

The reward datasets are constructed from 22 simulated clients across 65 sessions and 1,885 conversation contexts. The one-step lookahead dataset, \(D_1\), contains five scored candidate responses per context, giving \(1{,}885 \times 5 = 9{,}425\) examples. The two-step and three-step datasets, \(D_2\) and \(D_3\), are generated through beam-guided trajectory expansion and each contain four scored trajectories per context, giving \(1{,}885 \times 4 = 7{,}540\) examples per dataset. Together, the three datasets contain 24,505 examples. Table~\ref{tab:reward-dataset-summary} summarizes their composition.

The reward model is trained as a regression model. Its objective is to predict a scalar therapeutic reward score for each candidate response or lookahead trajectory. The reward targets are generated by the reward judge based on therapeutic dimensions such as therapeutic alliance, understanding and empathy, collaboration and structure, CBT skill and strategy, and MI quality and readiness. The reward model is fitted on the training groups, with the validation groups used for model selection and the held-out test groups used for final evaluation. Mean squared error loss is used for reward model training because the reward target is represented as a continuous scalar score.

\subsubsection{Supervised Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:sft-training-configuration}

The SFT policy is initialized from LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct. Before training, the selected response examples are balanced to reduce MI overrepresentation while preserving CBT examples. Each of the four MI techniques---Affirmation, Open Questioning, Reflective Listening, and Summarization---is randomly capped at 1,000 samples. Techniques with fewer available examples retain those examples. All selected CBT samples are retained without a cap. Sampling uses a fixed random seed of 42 for reproducibility. This procedure caps the MI categories rather than imposing an equal number of examples for every MI and CBT technique.

SFT uses LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct with a maximum sequence length of 1,024 tokens. Training runs for one epoch with a learning rate of \(2\times10^{-5}\) and 16 gradient accumulation steps. Low-rank adaptation uses a rank of 8 and a LoRA alpha of 32, with bfloat16 (BF16) numerical precision.

The resulting checkpoint is evaluated as AIM-RGL SFT and provides the starting policy for the subsequent GRPO variants. The shared policy prompt and required technique-response JSON format are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:policy-prompt}.

\subsubsection{GRPO Policy Optimization}

GRPO starts from the supervised fine-tuned LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct policy. Training contexts are prepared by extracting conversation histories from the reward dataset and removing the original candidate therapist response. The policy then samples five new candidate responses for each context; these five responses form the GRPO comparison group. Each generated completion is limited to 128 tokens.

The trained reward model remains frozen during policy optimization. Each candidate receives its reward model score plus a format compliance term of \(+0.2\) when all validation checks pass or \(-0.2\) otherwise. The format reward magnitude is therefore 0.2. The checks enforce valid two-field JSON, an allowed technique name, a non-empty response of at most 80 words, and the absence of extra text or example placeholders. The total reward follows Equations~\ref{eq:format-compliance-reward} and~\ref{eq:total-policy-reward}. GRPO compares rewards within each five-response group, computes relative advantages, and updates the policy to favor higher-reward responses. KL regularization limits deviation from the fixed reference policy initialized from the SFT checkpoint.

Training runs for one epoch with a learning rate of \(5\times10^{-7}\), a KL coefficient \(\beta=0.01\), and 10 gradient accumulation steps. LoRA is enabled with a rank of 8 and an alpha of 32, and computation uses bfloat16 (BF16) precision. The 128-token completion limit controls generation length, while the separate 80-word check determines format compliance.

The reward model and policy optimization stages are separated to make the experimental comparison clearer. The reward model training stage evaluates whether the system can learn to approximate therapeutic reward scores from lookahead data. The GRPO stage then evaluates whether this learned reward signal can improve the therapeutic response generation policy.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation-metric-scoring}

The definitions and theoretical basis of CTRS, MITI, WAI-O, and WAI-O-S are reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:therapeutic-dialogue-metrics}. This experiment applies LLM-based scoring to simulated conversations using the following procedures. Higher values indicate stronger performance within each metric.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CTRS:} Rate 11 items from 0 to 6 and report their arithmetic mean.

    \item \textbf{MITI:} Rate the four core global dimensions from 1 to 5 and report their mean. The additional direction rating requested by the evaluation prompt is excluded from this aggregate. This is an adapted summary score rather than a standard MITI 4 composite.

    \item \textbf{WAI-O:} Rate 36 items from 1 to 7, orient negatively worded items so that higher scores indicate stronger alliance, and average item scores across observer evaluations.

    \item \textbf{WAI-O-S:} Apply an adapted 1--5 rubric to 12 items and report the mean of their normalized scores. Reverse-coded items are reoriented before averaging. The 1--5 scale is specific to this implementation.
\end{itemize}

Scores are compared between systems within each metric. Their raw magnitudes are not directly comparable across metrics because the ranges and item sets differ. These automated adaptations have not been independently validated through clinical expert ratings, so the scores describe performance under the implemented rubrics rather than established clinical effectiveness.

\subsection{Statistical Comparison Procedure}
\label{sec:paired-permutation-tests}

Two statistical procedures are used for different comparison families. Tables~\ref{tab:main-results-easy}--\ref{tab:main-results-hard} use two-sided paired t-tests for comparisons against the D3 reference model, while Figures~\ref{fig:training-stage-heatmap} and~\ref{fig:lookahead-comparisons} use two-sided exact paired permutation tests. Their test statistics and multiple-comparison families are described separately below.

\textbf{Paired t-tests for the main result tables.}
Each of the six other models is compared with AIM-RGL SFT+GRPO(D3) on matched client observations within the same difficulty setting. The paired t-test evaluates whether the population mean within-pair score difference is zero. For differences \(d_i=y_{i,D3}-y_{i,A}\), its statistic is
\begin{equation}
t=\frac{\bar d}{s_d/\sqrt{n}},
\end{equation}
where \(s_d\) is the sample standard deviation of the paired differences and \(n\) is the number of matched pairs. The two-sided p-value is obtained from a t distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom \cite{scipyPairedTTest}.

Pairing is appropriate because the models respond to matched client scenarios, making their scores dependent within each pair. Analyzing within-pair differences accounts for variation between client cases and directly tests mean score differences. A two-sided test is used because D3 may score either higher or lower than another model. The validity of the t reference distribution depends on independent pairs and approximately normally distributed within-pair differences, particularly with small samples; severe outliers can also affect the result. This normality assumption concerns the paired differences, rather than requiring equal variances between the two models, and is not established merely because the ratings have been averaged.

Holm correction is applied jointly across \(6 \times 4 \times 3=72\) comparisons: six models against D3, four metrics, and three difficulty settings. Thus, the 72 tests form one family across all three tables. The correction controls the family-wise error rate at 0.05 and addresses the increased chance of false positives from repeated testing \cite{statsmodelsMultipleTests}. Asterisks identify Holm-adjusted significant differences from D3, with the score means indicating their direction.

\textbf{Exact paired permutation tests for the comparison figures.}
The comparisons in Figures~\ref{fig:training-stage-heatmap} and~\ref{fig:lookahead-comparisons} use two-sided exact paired permutation tests, implemented as sign-flip tests of the mean within-group score difference. Each model is evaluated on matched patient groups, so the unit of analysis is the patient group rather than an individual utterance or an independently sampled conversation. Pairing controls for differences between client cases and preserves the dependence between model scores for the same group.

For a comparison of models \(A\) and \(B\), let \(d_i=y_{i,B}-y_{i,A}\) be the score difference for patient group \(i\). The observed statistic is the mean difference, \(\bar d\). Its null distribution is constructed by independently reversing the sign of each paired difference:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathbf{s}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_i d_i,
\qquad s_i\in\{-1,+1\}.
\end{equation}
With \(n=20\), all \(2^{20}=1{,}048{,}576\) sign assignments are enumerated, giving an exact permutation distribution rather than a Monte Carlo approximation. Two-sided p-values assess departures in either direction and are computed using twice the smaller permutation-tail probability, capped at one \cite{scipyPermutationTest}.

This procedure is used because the sample contains only 20 matched groups and the scores are bounded ratings whose paired differences are not assumed to be normally distributed. Unlike a paired t-test, the sign-flip test does not rely on a normal-theory reference distribution for the mean difference. Exact enumeration is feasible at this sample size. However, the test is not assumption-free: it requires independent patient groups and exchangeability of the two model labels within each pair under the null, equivalently a null distribution invariant to independent sign reversals. Normality is unnecessary, but the sign-symmetry assumption remains important \cite{scipyPermutationTest}.

For the permutation-test figures, Holm correction controls the family-wise error rate at 0.05 within each of two separate families: the 36 GRPO--SFT tests and the 36 comparisons between lookahead depths. These families are separate from the 72 paired t-tests reported in the main result tables. Each family contains three model contrasts, four metrics, and three difficulty settings. The figures report adjusted p-values. A significant result indicates a difference in paired scores; its direction is determined by the sign of the mean difference.

This section reports the reward model backbone evaluation, followed by therapeutic response quality across baseline and trained policy variants. Standard has the highest easy-mode CTRS score (4.08), while AIM-RGL SFT has the highest normal- and hard-mode CTRS scores (3.43 and 3.34). MI fidelity also varies by setting: D2 has the highest easy-mode MITI score (4.41), SFT has the highest normal-mode score (4.15), and D3 has the highest hard-mode score (4.09).

Therapeutic alliance shows a clearer pattern. D3 has the highest WAI-O and WAI-O-S means in all three difficulty settings: 6.84 and 4.06 in easy mode, 6.73 and 3.97 in normal mode, and 6.57 and 3.92 in hard mode. D2 also exceeds SFT and D1 on both alliance measures in every setting. However, D1 does not consistently improve on the SFT policy, showing that GRPO alone does not guarantee stronger therapeutic scores.

Increasing lookahead depth is associated with lower therapeutic competence among the GRPO variants. CTRS decreases from D1 to D3 in all difficulty settings: from 3.67 to 2.80 in easy mode, from 3.40 to 2.28 in normal mode, and from 3.31 to 2.32 in hard mode. Thus, the alliance gains coincide with a reduction in CBT competence, while MI fidelity changes less consistently. These findings provide partial support for H1 and support the expectation in H2 that response quality differs across lookahead depths. They indicate a trade-off rather than uniform improvement across therapeutic dimensions. The significance annotations compare AIM-RGL SFT+GRPO(D3) against each other model within the same difficulty setting and metric using two-sided paired t-tests, with Holm correction across all 72 table comparisons. An asterisk on another model's score marks a significant difference from D3; the relative means determine whether D3 scores higher or lower. These annotations do not test differences between pairs of non-D3 models.

The one-step GRPO variant does not differ significantly from SFT on any of the four metrics in any difficulty setting after Holm correction. These results provide no evidence of a consistent improvement from one-step GRPO over SFT; lack of significance does not establish equivalence.

The two-step variant produces mixed changes. It significantly reduces CTRS in normal mode ($-0.532$, adjusted $p=0.033$) and hard mode ($-0.795$, adjusted $p=0.001$), while significantly increasing WAI-O only in normal mode ($+0.190$, adjusted $p=0.033$). Its WAI-O-S changes are not significant in any setting; the easy-mode increase of $+0.142$ has an adjusted $p=0.052$. None of its MITI differences from SFT is significant.

The three-step variant has significantly lower CTRS than SFT in all settings: $-0.918$ in easy, $-1.150$ in normal, and $-1.018$ in hard mode. It significantly improves WAI-O in all settings, with differences of $+0.431$, $+0.385$, and $+0.289$, respectively. MITI does not differ significantly from SFT for any GRPO variant in any difficulty setting.

These comparisons refine the interpretation of RQ1: GRPO does not uniformly improve on the supervised policy. D3 yields the clearest WAI-O gains, accompanied by lower CBT competence, while MI fidelity shows no significant improvement over SFT. The comparison against SFT also differs from the comparison against D1: alliance gains over a one-step GRPO policy do not necessarily imply significant gains over the SFT starting point.

\subsection{Quantifying Technique Diversity}

Technique diversity is summarized using the effective number of techniques:
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{16} p_k \ln p_k\right),
\label{eq:technique-diversity}
\end{equation}
where \(p_k\) is the share of technique \(k\) within a model, difficulty, and stage, normalized so the technique shares sum to one. Zero-share terms contribute zero. A value of one indicates complete concentration on one technique; a value of 16 indicates equal use of all 16 techniques. This measure accounts for both coverage and evenness, rather than merely counting nonzero techniques. The individual technique percentages used in this calculation are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:technique-heatmaps}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{figures/technique_diversity}
\caption{Effective number of techniques across conversation stages, calculated from all 16 individual technique shares. Values are approximate because they are calculated from percentages rounded to one decimal place. Higher values indicate a more evenly distributed technique mix, not necessarily better therapeutic responses.}
\label{fig:technique-diversity}
\end{figure}

D1 has the highest effective diversity in all nine difficulty--stage combinations, ranging from approximately 6.68 to 8.75 effective techniques. Its diversity decreases from early to late turns in all modes, consistent with the growing concentration on Decatastrophizing. SFT ranges from 5.45 to 7.46, D2 from 5.57 to 7.76, and D3 from 5.28 to 6.97. D2 and D3 are less diverse than D1, but diversity does not decrease monotonically with increasing lookahead depth: for example, D3 exceeds D2 in late-stage easy and normal modes, while the ordering reverses in early-stage hard mode.

These descriptive results address RQ3 and RQ4 and are consistent with the variation anticipated by H3 and H4. They do not constitute statistical tests of those hypotheses. Aggregate technique percentages alone do not establish statistical significance, causal effects, or a model's ability to adapt appropriately to individual clients. Likewise, the greater CBT share in D1 and stronger alliance scores in D2/D3 are compatible with the earlier response-quality trade-off, but aggregate technique frequencies do not establish that technique choice caused the score differences.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

DeBERTa-v3-large is selected as the reward model backbone because it achieves the lowest prediction errors and highest correlations among the evaluated backbones on \(D_1\). Using this reward model backbone, the dialogue evaluation shows that reward-guided policy optimization involves a trade-off between therapeutic alliance and therapeutic competence. The three-step GRPO variant achieves the highest mean WAI-O and WAI-O-S scores across all difficulty settings, while CBT competence generally declines as lookahead depth increases. MI fidelity varies across difficulty settings and training variants. Thus, the findings provide partial support for improved therapeutic response quality under RQ1, rather than a consistent improvement across all evaluated dimensions.

Direct comparisons with SFT show no significant differences for D1, mixed results for D2, and significant WAI-O gains accompanied by lower CTRS for D3 across all difficulty settings. None of the GRPO variants significantly improves MITI over SFT after Holm correction.

The paired comparisons clarify the effect of lookahead depth under RQ2. Two-step lookahead significantly improves both alliance measures over one-step lookahead in every difficulty setting and improves MI fidelity in easy mode. Three-step lookahead provides no statistically significant additional improvement over two-step lookahead in either alliance measure or MI fidelity within any difficulty setting, while reducing CBT competence significantly in easy and normal modes. These findings suggest that two-step lookahead offers a potential compromise between alliance gains and competence, although it does not eliminate the trade-off.

Technique-selection results address RQ3 by showing a distinct pattern for the one-step GRPO variant: it allocates approximately 50--60\% of responses to CBT and has the highest effective technique diversity across all difficulty--stage combinations. The two-step and three-step variants are predominantly MI-oriented. However, diversity does not decrease monotonically with depth, indicating that deeper lookahead changes the technique mix without producing a uniform narrowing of technique use.

For RQ4, technique choices vary across early, middle, and late conversation stages and across client difficulty settings. The one-step variant increasingly selects Decatastrophizing while reducing Pros and Cons Analysis, whereas the two-step variant shifts toward Open Questioning from early to late turns in all difficulty modes. The three-step variant exhibits different CBT-share trajectories across difficulty settings. These patterns indicate that aggregate technique shares can conceal changes over the course of a conversation. The technique analyses are descriptive and do not establish statistical significance or show that technique selection causes the observed therapeutic scores.

Overall, the evaluation indicates that greater lookahead depth should not be treated as uniformly better. Its benefits depend on the therapeutic dimension being prioritized, the client difficulty, and the stage of the conversation.
